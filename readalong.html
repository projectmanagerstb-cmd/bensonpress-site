Sammy

CAMDEN, NEW JERSEY | MONDAY, AUGUST 21, 1961 | 5:20 A.M.

Listen: Chapter One, read by the author

At twenty minutes past five, Pete’s trombone woke Sammy before the sun. The first note came through the floorboards low and unfinished, more vibration than music. Then another. A pause. The same phrase again. Wrong. Stop. Start over. That was Pete.

Samuel Edward Benson Jr. never called a job finished because he was tired of it. Whether he was tracing a dead circuit, fitting a piece of wire into a box nobody would ever see, or trying to make four bars of music sound the way they sounded in his head, Pete stayed with a thing until it was finished. Sammy lay still and listened. The Benson house on Berkley Street was dark, but darkness never meant silence. Eight children made silence mostly theoretical. Somebody breathed heavily across the room. Bed springs complained. A little foot hit the hallway floor, followed by another.

Sammy counted without opening his eyes. Three. Two. One. “Get back in that bed.” Viv’s voice came from the hall. The footsteps stopped.

“But Mama—”

“Don’t you ‘but Mama’ me, Samuel. You heard what I said.”

A beat of silence. Then quick little feet retreated the way they had come. Sammy smiled into his pillow. Nothing escaped Viv. He threw off the blanket, found the cold floor with his feet, and stood carefully. In the Benson house, you learned early to watch where you put your feet. There was a shoe near the bed, a shirt under it, and one of his younger brothers or sisters asleep crossways where nobody had been when the lights went out. Sammy stepped over all of it. He had no fancy name for the habit. He just knew that stepping on somebody meant hearing about it before breakfast.

Downstairs, Pete sat near the front room window with the trombone resting against his shoulder. Even half dressed for the day, he handled the horn with the same care he gave his tools. Pete noticed Sammy in the doorway.

“You planning on standing there all morning?”

“I ain’t bothering you.”

“You watching me.”

“So?”

“So people who watch ought to pay admission.”

Sammy leaned against the frame. “Your own son?”

“Especially my own son. You know how many mouths I got to feed in this house?”

Sammy grinned. “Whose fault is that?”

Pete slowly lowered the trombone. Sammy immediately understood that he had wandered onto dangerous ground. “Say that again.”

“Nah.”

“No. You had plenty to say a second ago.”

“I forgot.”

Pete nodded. “Smart boy.” He raised the horn again. “Listen.”

Pete played the phrase. A few slow notes, warm and heavy, not sad exactly but close enough to make Sammy feel something he could not name. Pete stopped. “What’s wrong with it?”

Sammy shrugged. “I don’t know.”

“I didn’t ask you to explain it, son. I asked what you hear.”

Pete played it again. Sammy listened harder. “The last part.”

“What about it?”

“Sounds like you’re trying too hard.”

Pete looked at him for a second, then nodded. “Exactly.”

He played it again. This time the last note came easier, like it had stopped fighting the song. Pete pointed the slide at Sammy.

“That’s why you listen before you talk.”

“Sometimes.”

“Every time.”

“You don’t.”

Pete stared at him. Sammy smiled. From the kitchen came Viv’s voice. “Samuel.”

Sammy straightened automatically. Pete’s mouth curled into a grin. “Yes, ma’am?”

“Come here.”

The kitchen was already turning into headquarters for the morning. Viv stood at the counter fixing breakfast with the speed of a woman who had long ago stopped measuring mornings by the clock. She measured them by children dressed, plates filled, quarrels stopped, and everybody pushed out the door on time. “You bothering your father?”

“No.”

Pete called from the other room. “Yes.”

Sammy looked toward the doorway. “See?”

Viv shook her head. “Both of y’all carrying on before breakfast. Lord, give me patience.” Pete started playing again.

Viv looked at Sammy. “You got practice today?”

“Yeah.”

“What time?”

“Morning and afternoon. Coach running two-a-days.”

“And you’re coming straight home afterward?”

Sammy hesitated. Viv turned. The hesitation ended. “Yes, ma’am.”

“That wasn’t what you were about to say.”

“Champ and Wayne was talking about doing something.”

“What something?”

“We don’t know yet.”

“Then you ain’t going nowhere till you can tell me what it is.”

“Mama.”

“You just told me you don’t know what it is.”

“We were gonna figure it out.”

“Figure it out after you come home.”

Sammy sighed. Viv’s eyes narrowed. He stopped. Being eighteen meant Sammy regularly felt like a grown man right up until his mother looked at him a certain way. Then eighteen became eight with astonishing speed. “You’re the oldest,” she said.

There it was. Sammy looked toward the ceiling as if asking God to review the arrangement. Viv caught that too.

“Don’t look up there. He ain’t getting you out of this.”

Pete laughed from the front room. Sammy muttered, “Always me.”

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That better be what you said.”

The rest of the house began waking around them. A door opened upstairs. Then another. Somebody complained that somebody else had taken something that belonged to them. A younger child yelled. Another yelled louder. One of the girls appeared in the kitchen doorway already prepared to present a case. “Mama, she took my—”

“I don’t care who took what. Both of you get dressed.”

“But—”

“Now.”

She disappeared. Viv looked at Sammy. “See?”

He knew exactly where this was going. “No.”

“You don’t even know what I’m gonna say.”

“Yes I do.”

“What?”

“I’m the oldest.”

Pete laughed so hard in the other room he missed a note.

Viv pointed through the doorway. “And don’t think I don’t hear you either, Pete.”

The trombone stopped. For one brief moment even Pete knew when to keep quiet. Then everybody went back to what they were doing. Sammy reached for a piece of food on the counter. Viv slapped his hand without looking. “You know better.”

“I was just—”

“You were just stealing breakfast.”

“Can you steal food from your own house?”

“If I ain’t put it on your plate yet, yes.”

Alva appeared with her hair pinned back and an expression that meant she had already settled two arguments upstairs. Barely a year behind Sammy, she could make their younger brothers and sisters move faster with one raised eyebrow than he could with a whole speech. She took the plates Viv handed her, counted heads without looking obvious about it, and told Jackie to put the school shoes back where she had found them.

“Wasn’t taking them,” Jackie said, holding one behind her back.

“Then I expect they can walk themselves right back upstairs,” Alva answered.

Helen came through humming the hymn the church choir had been working on. She was fourteen, old enough to believe that an early prayer improved the entire house and young enough to be offended when Teddy tried to sing harmony in the wrong key. Marsha followed with a book tucked beneath her arm and asked whether anybody had seen the pencil she had left on the windowsill. Sammy had noticed that the same book came downstairs three days running and that Marsha somehow managed to find a new question in it every morning.

Hope, who could turn a trip to the icebox into the beginning of a dance, caught Teddy by both hands and tried to make him move to the rhythm of Pete’s trombone. Teddy resisted only long enough to improve the beat by slapping it against the table. The two were near enough in age to quarrel like rivals and make peace before anybody had time to separate them. Shawn, the youngest at the time, sat cross-legged near the kitchen door, singing whatever part of the tune he could remember. Every few notes he invented a better one.

Viv looked over the room—the dishes, the missing shoes, the open book, the attempted dancing—and pointed at Sammy. “There. You see why I keep telling you you’re the oldest?”

“I ain’t started a one of these troubles,” he said.

“Wasn’t asking who started it. I asked whether you intend to help.”

Alva passed him a stack of plates. Sammy took them, and for a moment she gave him the sort of private smile that belonged only to two children who had been helping their mother since they were small enough to need a stool at the sink. Pete entered carrying the trombone. “Boy asked a reasonable question.”

Viv looked at him. Pete reconsidered. “Never mind.”

Sammy laughed. There were mornings he wanted out of that house more than anything in the world. Too many people. Too much noise. Too little privacy. Somebody always touching something that belonged to him. Somebody always needing something. But there were mornings like this one too, when the chaos had a rhythm to it. Viv. Pete. Children arguing. Pots. Doors. Laughter. The trombone. Home.

He did not know enough yet to understand that ordinary sounds become sacred after a man has heard enough gunfire. That morning, watching Pete carry the trombone upstairs and come back down in his work clothes, Sammy found himself thinking about the lives his father had carried before this one. The musician disappeared. The electrician took his place. Pete could leave the house with a toolbox in the morning and a trombone case at night, belonging completely to both worlds without asking either one for permission. Sammy had grown up seeing those two sides of him. What he understood less were the lives that had come before Camden.

Pete had been born upriver in Beverly, where the Delaware shaped the pace of life. He still talked about the river differently than Camden people did. To Pete, the water was not scenery. It was weather, work, fishing, tides, docks, men coming home dirty, and boys learning that the river could be peaceful one minute and dangerous the next. From Beverly he had made his way to Riverton, where older tradesmen taught him electricity. He had learned to work inside walls, crawl beneath houses, open fuse boxes, trace dead circuits, and respect current that could not be seen but could kill a careless man all the same.

Later he built a little business of his own, Benson Electrical Services. Nothing fancy. One man, his tools, his word, and the belief that if somebody paid him to fix a thing, they should not have to call him back because he had cut corners. The Army had come before much of that. Pete did not talk about his military service often. Sammy knew the photographs. Knew the old uniform. Knew the posture Pete still carried when he dressed well. He knew enough to understand that his father had been asked to serve the country before the country had learned how to respect men who looked like him. That lesson was not delivered in a speech. It lived in moments.

Sammy had seen white men call Pete by his first name while calling a white tradesman “Mister.” He had watched customers look past his father toward a younger white helper as though the helper must be the man in charge. He had seen people compliment Pete’s work with the faint surprise of somebody who had expected less.

Pete rarely gave them the satisfaction of watching him shrink. He corrected what needed correcting and kept moving. Once, when Sammy was younger, he had ridden with Pete on a Saturday electrical call. A white homeowner opened the door, looked at Pete, then at the white apprentice who had come along to carry materials.

“You the electrician?” the man asked the apprentice.

The apprentice started to answer. Pete beat him to it. “No. I am.”

The homeowner looked Pete over. “You?” Pete’s face did not change.

“You called Benson Electrical Services. I’m Benson. Now, you want those lights fixed, or you paying me to stand on this porch?”

The apprentice stared at the ground to hide a smile. The homeowner stepped aside. Pete fixed the problem. He charged exactly what he had quoted. On the ride home, Sammy waited for him to say something about the man’s attitude. Pete did not.

Finally Sammy asked, “Why you let him talk to you like that?”

Pete kept his eyes on the road. “Let him?”

“He thought—”

“I know what he thought.”

“That don’t make you mad?” Pete looked at him then.

“Course it makes me mad. Anger ain’t the question. What a man does with it—that’s the question.”

Sammy had no answer. Pete tapped the steering wheel.

“Man wanted his lights fixed. I fixed his lights. He knows who did it now.”

“That’s it?”

“No. That’s not it. But everything don’t deserve all of you. Learn that early.”

Then, after a moment, Pete added, “And don’t you ever give another man your dignity trying to make him see yours.”

Sammy remembered that. He did not always follow it. But he remembered it. Pete’s steady job at RCA eventually gave the family the kind of stability that independent electrical work alone could not promise. He spent years inside the plant keeping machines, lines, motors, circuits, and production systems alive. He became known as the man people called when somebody else had already tried and failed. At home, he treated electricity almost like a moral system.

“Understand where the power coming from before you touch anything,” he told Sammy. “Otherwise it’ll teach you.”

Sammy once asked what electricity would teach him. Pete looked at him. “Not to touch it twice.”

That was Pete. Instruction hidden inside humor. He checked his toolbox now before leaving. Every tool had a place. Nothing thrown in carelessly. Nothing assumed. At the door he turned toward Sammy. “Practice.”

“I know.”

“Knowing ain’t doing.”

“I’ll be there.” Pete nodded.

Then, almost as an afterthought, he said, “If you go, go all the way. Don’t half-ass anything.”

Sammy had heard versions of it before. Football. School. Work. Chores. Whatever the subject, Pete’s rule remained the same. If you committed, commit. Pete opened the door. “And help your mother.”

Sammy frowned. “What?”

Pete smiled. “You the oldest.”

The door shut before Sammy could answer. Outside, Berkley Street was coming alive. Camden mornings did not ease themselves into the day. Doors opened. Men left for work. Women swept stoops. Children appeared in doorways with unfinished breakfasts. Cars coughed awake. Somewhere a radio played through an open window. Sammy stepped outside and sat on the brick stoop for a minute before practice. This was the country as Sammy knew it: a whole block waking up to work.

Not the America from textbooks with marble buildings and presidents staring out from portraits. Not yet. His America was Berkley Street. It was the man down the block who had served and never talked about it. The woman who knew every child on the street and would tell your mother exactly what she saw you doing. The father carrying a lunch pail. The mother stretching one chicken across too many plates. The veteran who could wear ribbons in a parade and still be followed around a store by somebody suspicious of his skin. It was dignity without applause. Hard work that rarely became history.

Black men like Pete did not wake every morning thinking of themselves as symbols. They had bills. They had children. They had shifts to make. They had things to fix. America benefited from their labor whether America noticed them or not. That was one of the first truths Sammy inherited. Another was that Pete never taught him to surrender the country because of it. Sammy could still hear something his father had told him after another conversation about race, years earlier.

“Don’t let nobody hand you less America than they keep for themselves.”

Pete had not said it like a slogan. He had been tightening a fitting when he said it, barely looking up. That made Sammy remember it even more. The Bensons did not believe they were guests in the United States. Pete had worn the uniform. He worked in the factories. He paid taxes. He raised children. He played American music in American clubs for American audiences that sometimes loved the sound more than they respected the men making it. None of that made him more American. It simply proved what should never have needed proving. The country belonged to him too. A horn sounded at the corner. Sammy stood. Time for practice.

He grabbed his jacket and headed toward the meeting point near Kaighn and Broadway. The walk took him through a Camden that outsiders would later describe mostly through decline, unrest, and everything that went wrong. That was not the Camden Sammy knew at eighteen. His Camden had motion.

Broadway carried people in both directions beneath storefront signs. Corner shops opened early. Men stood outside barbershops talking baseball, work, and whatever those fellows down in Washington were doing now. Record stores put new 45s where youngsters could see them through the glass. Doo-wop still had a hold, jazz belonged to men like Pete and the musicians who came through the house, and rhythm and blues was pushing into something new. Sammy, Wayne, and Champ did not need anybody to explain it. If a record sounded good, it sounded good. And they knew where young people could be found: Broadway, Kaighn Avenue, the parks, the Y.

Church socials that boys complained about until they found out girls were attending. The Sugar Bowl, where a milkshake could last as long as a conversation if a boy did not have enough money to order another one. The movie houses, where darkness made courage easier and a young man might finally put his arm around a girl after spending twenty minutes pretending not to think about it. Farnham Park belonged to everybody depending on the hour. Basketball. Baseball. Pickup football. Family gatherings. Arguments that began over a foul and ended with everybody laughing twenty minutes later.

Camden was not waiting around to become a chapter in somebody else’s history book. People were living. Wayne was already near the corner when Sammy arrived. He was talking, naturally. Wayne could turn being alone into a conversation if given enough time. Kaighn Avenue had trained him well. Traffic, stores, porches, passing neighbors, men leaning in doorways, people talking over one another. Wayne moved through all of it like the city had been built to provide him an audience.

“Look who decided to show up,” Wayne said.

“I ain’t late.”

“You ain’t early either.”

“Where Champ?”

“Probably fixing his hair.”

“You jealous because yours can’t be fixed.”

Wayne touched his head. “This is intentional.”

“That’s worse.”

Champ arrived from the direction of Maple Avenue before Wayne could answer. Where Wayne brought noise with him, Champ carried quiet. Maple had shaped him differently. Families knew one another. Church people knew whose child belonged to whom. Trouble had a hard time arriving anonymously. Champ was smooth on the football field and off it, though he would never admit he worked at either. “Y’all arguing already?”

“He started it,” Wayne said.

Sammy looked at him. “We grown.”

“And?”

“You still sound six.”

Champ laughed. The three fell into step. Three streets. Three homes. Three versions of Camden. One friendship.

They passed a storefront where music drifted through the doorway. Wayne slowed immediately. “Hear that?” Champ nodded. Sammy kept moving.

Wayne called after him. “Man, you got no soul.”

“I got practice.”

“Same thing wrong with you.” Champ caught up beside Sammy.

“He gonna be late one day because of a record.”

“He gonna be late to his own funeral because somebody playing a good song outside.”

Wayne jogged after them. “At least mine gonna have music. Yours gonna sound like study hall.”

They laughed all the way to the bus. Years later, that was what Sammy remembered most clearly about mornings like that. Three boys laughing. Race was in their lives, sure enough, but it was not all they talked about and it was not all they were. They talked football more than Washington, girls more than speeches, records more than court decisions. They worried about Coach Ulmer, Friday night’s opponent, whether somebody’s mama would let him out after the game, and which one of them had made a fool of himself trying to talk to a girl. The country was changing around them. The boys were busy being boys. Still, they noticed things. They had to.

A newspaper rack might carry another story about demonstrations down South. Adults might lower their voices over coffee when they talked about Negro students trying to enter places somebody had decided were not meant for them. Ministers spoke about dignity. Veterans spoke about what they had fought for. At the barbershop, men argued over whether patience was wisdom or just a polite name for waiting. Sammy listened more than he spoke. Pete had trained him for that without realizing it. Listen before you talk. Watch before you move.

By the time the bus carried Sammy, Wayne, and Champ away from Camden, the streets outside the windows began changing. The shift was not dramatic enough for somebody unfamiliar with the area to notice immediately. Sammy noticed. Different houses. More space between them. Different storefronts. Different faces. Less noise. Palmyra. Only a few miles away. Same country. Different feel.

Nobody greeted them at the schoolhouse door with hatred. That would have been simpler. Most days were ordinary. Teachers taught. Students complained. Boys lied about girls. Girls pretended not to hear them. Football players worried about Friday night. But Camden followed the three boys into the building even when nobody said the word. Sometimes it appeared as curiosity. Sometimes admiration. Sometimes distance. Sometimes a joke. Sometimes a look that stayed half a second too long. Sammy had learned not to confuse every look with an enemy. Pete had taught him that too. Real enemies were enough work.

But Sammy also knew when somebody had already made up his mind. “Where you from?”

“Camden.” Then the little pause. “Oh.”

There were a hundred ways a person could say oh. Sammy was learning to hear every one of them. Football helped. A block was a block. A catch was a catch. A touchdown counted six no matter what street the runner slept on. Coach Ulmer cared about assignments, hustle, wind, and whether eleven boys did what they were told to do. Sammy liked that. Pete did too. Electricity either ran or it did not. A circuit worked or it failed. A man finished a job or he half-assed it. Football made the same kind of sense.

Sammy was built for it. Broad shoulders. Long arms. Strong legs. By senior year he looked less like a schoolboy than a young man somebody had put in shoulder pads. Wayne said he was built like one of those African warriors you saw in the magazines.

“Nigerian warrior,” Champ corrected one day. “Nigeria got its freedom last year. Read something besides the sports page.”

The phrase stuck among the three of them. Sammy pretended not to like it. Truth was, he did. But size was only part of what made him good. Sammy read a defense. He noticed how a linebacker leaned before the snap, which safety crept toward the line, which end was getting tired and starting to stand up between plays. He could not have explained how he knew. He just saw things. Coach Ulmer saw that too. So did his friends. Nobody had given that habit a military name. Not yet.

Linda was not a stranger who had suddenly learned his name because the football season had begun. The three boys had known her since the spring, when Palmyra held a dance in the school gym and Wayne decided that he was the best-looking boy in Burlington County. He announced this conclusion before they got off the bus. Champ told him he ought to have his eyes examined. Sammy told him there were probably county ordinances against boasting that much in public.

Linda had been helping set out refreshments when they arrived. She wore a blue dress with a narrow belt, and she was standing on a chair trying to fasten a string of crepe paper to a basketball support. Wayne saw her and smoothed the front of his jacket so quickly that Sammy almost missed it.

“Evening, Miss Carver,” Wayne said. “A young lady ought not to be up there doing dangerous work.”

Linda looked down. “Then hold the chair instead of making speeches.”

Sammy took one side before Wayne could answer. Wayne took the other. Linda finished the paper, stepped down, and looked from one of them to the other. “Thank you, Sammy. Thank you, Wayne.”

“You knew our names?” Wayne asked.

“We’ve been going to the same school for three years, Wayne.”

Champ, standing near the punch bowl, nearly choked on his drink. Wayne recovered as he always did: by talking. He asked Linda for the first dance, and she said yes. Then he asked for another. She said yes to that one too. By the time the evening was halfway over, the entire football team had decided Wayne had a girl. Wayne encouraged this misunderstanding whenever Linda was out of earshot.

But Sammy saw the part of the evening Wayne did not. During a slower record, Linda asked where Wayne thought he would be the following year. Wayne promised he would have a fine car, a good job, and no shortage of company. She laughed, because Wayne could make anybody laugh, then told him she wanted to teach school. She had been looking at Glassboro State College and was worried about money, grades, and whether her family would think the trip too far. Wayne said she’d make a pretty teacher. Linda smiled politely. The answer had not been what she asked for.

Near the end of the dance, Sammy found her in the hallway carrying an empty punch bowl toward the kitchen. He took the heavier end without asking. For a few steps neither spoke. Then she asked him whether he thought Wayne meant half the things he said.

“About half,” Sammy answered. “He means the good half.”

She laughed. He liked her laugh, but he liked it even more when she began talking again about college. Sammy asked what age she wanted to teach. Linda told him she liked the little ones before they learned to be ashamed of asking questions. She talked about a teacher who had once lent her books and stayed after school when she was having trouble with mathematics. She wanted to do that for somebody else.

He had never heard Wayne speak about his future without making a joke of it. Linda spoke as though there was work to be done and she meant to get on with it. Sammy understood that kind of talk. It reminded him of Pete making a list before he opened an electrical panel.

Wayne caught up with them at the door. He had saved Linda the last dance. Sammy handed over the bowl and stepped aside. He was not about to come between his friend and a girl who had agreed to dance with him. The following week Wayne bought her a soda after school and walked her to her ride. He told the boys the matter was all but settled. But when he asked her for Saturday, she said she had promised her mother she would help at home, and when he asked again she told him kindly that they were better off as friends. Wayne laughed it off in front of the team. Sammy knew the laughter was doing some work.

By August, Wayne’s pride had recovered, at least when Linda was nowhere nearby. He teased Sammy about her because he had already noticed what Sammy was trying to hide. That made the business delicate. Sammy liked Wayne too well to make a contest of it, and he liked Linda too much to pretend he did not look for her in the hall.

Recon had not entered Sammy’s life. At the lunch break, Wayne was halfway through a story nobody believed when Linda Carver walked past their table carrying books against her chest. “Good luck in the scrimmage Friday, Sammy.”

Sammy looked up too late to prepare anything resembling charm. “Thanks.”

Linda smiled and kept walking. Wayne slowly put down his fork. Champ looked at Sammy. Neither said anything. Sammy pointed at them. “Don’t.” Wayne’s face broke first.

“That’s what you gave her? Thanks?”

“Shut up.”

Champ shook his head. “Man had all morning to learn English.”

“Both of y’all shut up.”

Wayne leaned back. “Nigerian warrior until a girl talk to him.”

Sammy kicked him under the table. The three laughed loud enough for somebody two tables over to turn around. For that minute the weight of Camden and Palmyra fell away. He was eighteen, a pretty girl had spoken to him, and his best friends would never let him forget how foolish he had looked.

The laughter followed him back onto the practice field. By the time Coach Ulmer blew the last whistle, Linda’s smile and Wayne’s teasing had settled into the same place as everything else waiting for senior year to begin. Football would demand his attention tomorrow. For now, Wayne and Champ were still beside him, and the whole year lay ahead.
